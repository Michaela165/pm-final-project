Slide 5 · Strategy · Modules 1 + 3

A slow loss of position, not a sudden crisis

Problem hook

We must stop Lena from defaulting to rivals and her debit card by giving her one reliable, in-store-capable Riverty card that shows what she owes in a single place, so Riverty owns an everyday relationship instead of relying on whatever checkout happens to show.

If Riverty does nothing for 12 months: it stays dependent on merchants and on whichever option shows at checkout, while rivals build the direct consumer relationship it lacks.

Value proposition

For heavy BNPL users like Lena, an approved Riverty card that she can add to her phone wallet in one tap, so it works at the till and she no longer falls back to her debit card. Every month Riverty waits, plus the months of regulatory approval, keeps its growth dependent on whatever checkout happens to show.

Data-backed hypothesis

Based on the pilot, where 143 of 431 activated cards (33.2%) never reached the phone wallet (T-102) and N-03 taps a bank card in shops without thinking, I believe that an “Add to wallet” button on the approval screen will get more Lena-type users’ cards into their wallet, and so into in-store use, as measured by a +5pp absolute lift in wallet add rate versus control (my assumption). I will protect the in-store decline rate (8.7% in Month 4, boundary +2pp, my assumption) and make a single go/no-go call at a fixed read date.

Proven (data pack): 143 of 431, 33.2%, 8.7%. Assumed: +5pp, +2pp.

Slide 6 · Research · Module 2

Riverty becomes the thing to check, not the thing to use

Competitive analysis / workaround

Role
A heavy BNPL user who already runs two or more pay later apps and checks them before payday.
Goal
See in one place what they owe in total, and when, before payday.
Friction
They can’t see what they owe on the card and on regular pay later orders together (N-07). Balances sit on two screens (T-103), and two reminders for different amounts arrive in the same week (T-104).
Tools used
Klarna, PayPal, bank app, Riverty card and pay later orders. The persona checks several apps before payday (N-02).
Core frustration
Nothing links the totals, so the user can’t trust any single number (T-103). A third app feels like more work (N-02).

Evidence

  • One place: the card adds a screen to the checking routine instead of replacing one.
  • Debit fallback: when totals can’t be trusted, the safe option is the debit card, so Riverty loses everyday spending.
  • Checkout dependence stays: no direct habit with Riverty, the brief’s core risk.
  • Heavy users carry the risk: highest spend (EUR 320.88 monthly) and the most pay later products to track.

Journey map

Slide 7 · Blueprint · Module 4

Now: wallet and decline reason. Everything else waits.

Nowthis sprint

B1 · Decline reason and retry hint in the app

Hits the primary metric and N-06’s churn moment (T-101, 8.7% in-store declines in Month 4). Assumes decline codes are available to the app, not yet verified.

B2 · Add to phone wallet from the approval screen

143 activated cards never reached the wallet (T-102, 66.8% baseline). No wallet means no in-store use: a precondition for the in-store pitch.

Laterrevisit

B5 · Partner offers in the card app

The merchant-side story (N-09), but not Lena’s friction, and needs merchant integrations. Revisit for brief Q3, not for this test.

Cut: the “no” I’m protecting

B4 · Pay now or pay later per purchase

Instalments are only 15–18% of purchases, T-105 shows confusion rather than demand, and it needs per-purchase credit decisioning plus approval.

B6 · Cashback on every purchase

N-12: drives a first try, not continued use. Costs margin, needs pricing approval.

B7 · Physical card

N-03 taps a bank card by habit. The wallet (B2) already covers tapping; a card adds production and logistics.

PRD highlights · Must-haves for “Add to phone wallet straight from the approval screen”

  • Add to wallet button on the approval screen (Apple Pay and Google Pay), provisioned in-app, no re-entering card details.
  • Skippable. Must never block approval or activation (approved → activated is 73.5%, already a leak).
  • Failure state with a reason and a retry, so a silent failure doesn’t repeat the N-06 pattern.
  • State check: hide the button if the card is already in the wallet or the device can’t take it.
  • Funnel events: shown, tapped, started, succeeded, failed (error code), abandoned. Security and compliance baseline; PCI scope needs a check.

Slide 8 · Validation · Module 5

One change, one read, fixed date

Hypothesis

An “Add to wallet” primary button on the approval screen (FR-1 to FR-6) for Lena-type users (heavy and regular BNPL users, 25–44) will result in more cards reaching the phone wallet, and so more in-store use, as measured by a +5pp absolute change in wallet add rate within [N weeks = 2,660 activations ÷ weekly activations, minimum 2 weekly cycles].

Control (A) vs Variant (B)

  • A: after approval and activation, the person adds the card to their wallet on their own. 143 of 431 activated cards (33.2%) never reached it (T-102).
  • B: exactly one change: an “Add to phone wallet” action on the approval screen (B2). Skip never blocks approval; 3 failure reasons plus Retry; “Add later” after 3 failed retries.
  • Unchanged in both arms: app build, eligibility and credit policy, identity check, notifications, decline messaging (B1), balance view (B3), due dates (B8). Randomised per person at approval, 50/50.

Primary metric · baseline · MDE

Primary metric
Wallet add rate
Baseline
66.8% activated to wallet (n=431, 4-month pilot). Pilot is mostly employees.
MDE
+5pp absolute (66.8% to 71.8%), my assumption
Sample size
~1,330 per arm (α=0.05, 80% power, calculated by hand; check against the builder). At +10pp MDE: ~315.
Significance
p < 0.05, two-sided
Guardrail
In-store decline rate, baseline 8.7% (Month 4, 206 attempts). Investigate above ~10.7% (+2pp, my assumption). Noisy: 12.1% in Month 1, 6.4% in Month 3.

Open point: the 66.8% baseline measures activated cards to wallet, but the button sits before activation, so the eligible group changes. Measure a baseline for the new definition first.

Ship

Wallet add rate improves by ≥ 5pp at p < 0.05 and in-store decline rate does not reach 10.7%.

Iterate

Direction is positive but the lift is below 5pp.

Kill

No improvement, or it moves negatively. The read date is fixed; no results reviewed before it.

Bad signal

Wallet add rises but in-store use stays flat. People add the card but still pay with debit (N-03), so wallet add is a vanity metric. Don’t ship on wallet add alone.

Slide 10 · Story · Individual insights

The metric definition was the hard part

Friction points & the aha moment

The hardest part was defining the primary metric, not the MDE. The only wallet baseline in the data pack (66.8%) measures activated cards to wallet, but my button sits on the approval screen, before activation. That changes who can add the card, so the baseline no longer matched the test. Fixing it changed my hypothesis. It also drives the baseline, sample size, isolation check and shipping rule.

A second friction: the pilot is mostly employees (N-10), and no decline or drop-off data is split by segment. My link to Lena is inference, not proof.

Key takeaways

  • Define the metric and its denominator before setting the MDE. Everything else depends on it.
  • Keep proven facts (data pack) apart from my assumptions (+5pp lift, +2pp guardrail, p < 0.05).

What I’d do next

  • Measure a wallet add baseline for the approval-screen definition, or move the button after activation.
  • Persist arm-tagged events (FR-7 is an in-memory list today).
  • Ask compliance whether a wallet-add flow counts as a proposition change (N-11): it gates everything.
  • Run the test once the card reaches volume (about 1,330 per arm).

Most likely outcome: iterate (my judgement, not a forecast).
