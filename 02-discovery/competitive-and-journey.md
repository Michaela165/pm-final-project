# Competitive Analysis & Journey Map (Module 2)

## Responses
- **Role, who are you solving for? (the specific user segment or profile):** A heavy BNPL user who already runs two or more pay later apps and checks them before payday.
- **Goal, what is this user ultimately trying to achieve?:** See in one place what they owe in total, and when, before payday.
- **Friction, the main barrier (moment of misery) stopping them from succeeding:** They can’t see what they owe on the card and on regular pay later orders together (N-07). Balances sit on two screens (T-103), and two reminders for different amounts arrive in the same week (T-104).
- **External tools, the outside platforms or tools the user is forced to use:** The persona checks several apps before payday (N-02).
They can’t see card and pay later totals together (N-07, T-103).
Reminders arrive in the same week for different amounts (T-104).
Nothing in the pack says they use a spreadsheet, notes app, screenshots or bank app, or how they reconcile.
- **The process, the 3 to 5 manual steps the user takes to get the job done:** Open each pay later app (Riverty card, Riverty pay later orders, Klarna, PayPal) and read each balance and due date.
Copy the figures into a note, a spreadsheet or their head, or take screenshots.
Open the bank app to compare the total against the expected balance on payday.
Re-check after each reminder, because the amounts differ (T-104) and it isn’t clear which total is real (T-103).
Fall back to the debit card for purchases when unsure, as in N-06.
- **Core frustration, the exact moment the process feels most “broken”:** It is manual and repeated before every payday.
Nothing links the totals, so the user can’t trust any single number (T-103).
Differing reminders create doubt about which amount is due (T-104).
The effort adds a cost to every extra pay later product, so a third app feels like more work (N-02).
- **The evidence, a specific quote or behavior from the research that proves this:** Riverty becomes the thing to check, not the thing to use. The card adds a screen to the checking routine instead of replacing one, so it works against the “one place” part of the hook.
Debit card fallback: when the user can’t trust the totals, the safe option is the debit card, so Riverty loses everyday spending (the “defaulting to rivals and her debit card” part of the hook).
Checkout dependence stays: the user never forms a direct habit with Riverty, so they stay with whatever appears at checkout, which is the brief’s core risk.
Heavy users carry the risk. They have the highest spend (EUR 320.88 monthly) and the most pay later products to track, so the friction hits the segment that matters most.
